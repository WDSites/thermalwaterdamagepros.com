Water removal, also called water extraction, uses pumps and truck-mounted or portable extraction units to pull standing water out of a structure. It is the first step of any serious loss. Drying equipment and repairs come after the water itself is gone, which is covered under water mitigation.

When standing water is an emergency, and what to do while you wait

These are the same steps a dispatcher gives on the phone. Do them only if it is safe, and skip any step that puts you near electricity or contaminated water.

  • Stop the source

    Shut off the main water valve or the fixture valve. A running supply line keeps adding water faster than any pump removes it.

  • Stay clear of power

    If water touches outlets, appliances or the panel, do not wade in. Call the utility or 911 for electrical or gas risk before you call us.

  • Lift what you can

    Raise small furniture onto blocks, pull rugs and move electronics and papers to a dry room.

  • Keep people out

    Keep children and pets away from standing water, especially if it came from a drain, toilet or outdoors.

  • Take quick photos

    Photograph the water line and damaged items before anything is moved. Your insurer will ask for them.

  • Call the 24/7 line

    Give the dispatcher your cross streets, the water source and how deep the water is.

Electrical safety comes first

Never stand in water while touching a switch, outlet or appliance. Cut power at the panel only if the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping through water. If you smell gas or see sparks, leave and call 911.

How extraction works in a house, step by step

Crews bring pumps for deep water and extraction units that pull water from carpet, pad and hard floors. A moisture meter then checks what the visible water left behind, because the floor can look dry while the subfloor underneath is not.

Carpet and pad get special attention. If the water was clean and the response was quick, carpet can often be lifted, the pad removed, and the carpet dried in place. When the water was contaminated or sat for days, the pad and sometimes the carpet go to the dumpster.

Extraction ends with a handoff. The crew documents what was removed, sets drying equipment if the structure needs it, and tells you what to watch for over the next day. That written record also helps if you file a claim.

Burst pipe water damage: the first hour matters most

A burst pipe is the classic emergency. Supply lines release gallons a minute, and a break inside a wall can run for hours before anyone sees a stain. In a hot desert climate, pipes in attics and exterior walls are also stressed by heat and age, and a failed fitting can let go with no warning.

Shut off the main valve first, then open a low faucet to drain what remains in the line. Photograph the damage before moving anything. Do not use a household vacuum on standing water, and do not use appliances while the floor is wet.

The crew then finds the break, pumps and extracts, and opens the wall where readings show moisture has traveled. A plumber repairs the pipe itself. We handle the water, drying and the dry-out documentation your insurer will ask about.

What drives the cost of water removal

There is no useful price without seeing the water, so scope and price are explained before work starts. These are the factors.

  • 1
    Depth and volume more water means more pump time and more extraction passes.
  • 2
    Water category clean supply line water and contaminated drain water are handled and billed differently.
  • 3
    Square footage the number of rooms the water reached sets labor and equipment needs.
  • 4
    Materials pad, carpet, hardwood and drywall each have a different save-or-remove decision.
  • 5
    Time since the loss water that sat for days has soaked further and needs more removal and drying.

On insurance, sudden and accidental water damage from a burst pipe or failed appliance is often covered, and slow leaks often are not. Flood damage generally needs a separate flood policy. Ask your insurer before you rely on any assumption.
